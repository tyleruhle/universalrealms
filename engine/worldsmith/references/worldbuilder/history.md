# History & Timelines

History is the spine of a world. Every present-day institution, grievance, ruin, festival, and family name is a residue of past events. Build history not as trivia but as *why things are the way they are*.

## Top-down vs. bottom-up

Two valid approaches:

- **Top-down:** Start with broad eras, then fill in major events, then specific dates. Good when the world is new and you need shape fast.
- **Bottom-up:** Start with the present situation and ask "what would have had to happen for this?" Good when there's already a setting and you need to explain it. Decide the present by hand, but do not invent the chain backwards event by event; histories built in reverse contradict themselves the moment anyone checks (see `references/worldforge/living-world.md`). Instead, write the present as canon constraints and let saga search for a forward-simulated history that arrives there (`modules/saga.md`, Mode 1b: fit).

Most projects benefit from doing both — sketch the top-down shape, then bottom-up to anchor specific present-day details.

## Eras, not years

For most projects, **don't start with a year-by-year timeline.** Start with named eras, each with a distinct flavor:

- The First Age: gods walked the land
- The Long Twilight: gods withdrew; humans fumbled for self-rule
- The Iron Era: the great empires rose
- The Sundering: a cataclysm broke everything
- The Present Age (Year 412 since the Sundering): rebuilding, fractured

Each era should have a *texture* — what life felt like, what was possible then that isn't now (or vice versa). Eras are easier to remember and reason about than dates. Dates get added only where they matter.

## The shape of a history

Real histories aren't a straight march of progress. They have:

- **Rises and falls.** Empires grow, peak, decay, fragment, are succeeded.
- **Lost knowledge.** Things the past knew that the present doesn't. (How the road was built. How the script was read. How the medicine was made.)
- **Recurring patterns.** Cycles of consolidation and breakup, dark ages and renaissances.
- **Contingency.** Single events that changed everything and didn't have to happen. (One battle, one assassination, one plague carrier.)
- **Slow tectonic shifts.** Things that took centuries — climate change, religious conversion, technology adoption — that no one alive at the start lived to see finish.

A history that's only the rise-of-the-good-guys is a fairy tale; a history with all four of these textures feels real.

## The driving questions

Use these to generate history, not just record it:

- **Why is anyone in charge?** Trace the current power structure back. Who took it from whom, when, with whose help?
- **What are the grievances?** Every group has historical wounds that still matter politically. Build those.
- **Where are the ruins?** Old structures imply old civilizations. Who built them, why are they ruined, what's still there?
- **What are the borders, and who drew them?** Borders are almost always the residue of wars. Whose war?
- **What's the religion's history?** Reformations, schisms, persecutions, conversions, syncretic blends.
- **What was the worst thing?** Every culture has a remembered catastrophe — war, plague, famine, betrayal. What's this one's?
- **What was the golden age?** When (in collective memory) was it best? What did people lose? (Often the "golden age" wasn't actually that good — but the *belief* shapes the present.)

## Layers of memory

History exists in several forms at once:

1. **What actually happened** (the author/GM's truth, possibly unknown to anyone in-world)
2. **What's recorded** (chronicles, scriptures, official histories — usually written by winners)
3. **What's remembered** (folk memory, songs, oral tradition — often more accurate emotionally than factually)
4. **What's mythologized** (events transformed into stories that explain the world)
5. **What's forgotten or suppressed** (the deliberate or accidental gaps)

Saga's chronicle carries layers 1, 2 and 5 directly (`truth`, per-faction `accounts`, `suppressed`); layers 3 and 4 are authored on top of it. See `references/saga/historiography.md`.

The gaps and contradictions between these layers are gold. A people who remember they were victims when the chronicles say they were aggressors; a religion whose founding event the historians can't find evidence for; a battle everyone agrees happened but no one agrees who won.

## The right level of detail

Match the resolution to the use case:

- **Pure backdrop** (story set in present, history is flavor): 5-10 named eras, 10-20 named events, key dates only.
- **Active history** (story involves recovering the past, prophecies, ruins): add genealogies, named historical figures, specific century-level dates, lost knowledge.
- **Historical setting** (story set in a specific historical period of your world): full chronology for the relevant century, named generations, season-level events.

Don't over-detail eras the story won't touch. A vague "the First Age" is fine if no one's going to walk through it.

## Chronologies

When a real timeline is needed, format like this:

```
## The Iron Era (years 0–612 of the current count)

### Founding (yr 0–80)
- yr 0: The Compact of Kelmar — six cities swear mutual defense
- yr 23: First Iron Council convened
- yr 47: War of the Spires — northern cities seceded, were reconquered

### Expansion (yr 80–340)
- yr 92: Trade road to the Salt Coast opened
- yr 187: Conquest of the Marshlands; introduction of rice cultivation
- yr 241: First imperial census, 4.2 million subjects counted
...
```

Date format conventions: pick a year-zero and stick to it. Many real calendars count from a founding event, a religious event, or an arbitrary regnal year. State the convention once in `00-overview.md`.

## Genealogies

For political settings, royal/noble family trees often matter more than military history. They explain wars, alliances, claims, and assassinations. A succinct format:

```
## The Verani Dynasty
Marius I (b.231 d.287, r.255-287)
└─ Marius II (b.260 d.301, r.287-301) — assassinated; no legitimate heir
   └─ disputed; Elena Verani (cousin, b.254 d.319, r.301-319) seized throne
      ├─ Tomas I (b.279 d.330, r.319-330) — abdicated, took monastic vows
      │   └─ Tomas II (b.301 d.342, r.330-342) — d. in the Plague
      └─ Isolde Verani (b.282 d.345)
          └─ The current claimant line
```

Don't build genealogies for cultures the story won't use. A few key family trees go further than encyclopedic completeness.

## Common pitfalls

- **The cataclysm trap.** Every fantasy world has a cataclysm 500-1000 years ago that conveniently flattens prior history into vague mystery. Used once, it's fine. Used reflexively, it's lazy. Consider: what if the past *isn't* mostly destroyed? What if there's a continuous documentary record going back 2,000 years?
- **History as backdrop for the present.** Every past event is described in terms of how it caused the present situation. But people lived those eras; events had their own meaning then. Render eras as they felt to people inside them, not just as causes of now.
- **The hero-king disease.** History described mostly through great rulers. Add merchants, peasants, religious reformers, plagues, climate shifts, technological changes, migrations. Most history is not made by kings.
- **Forgetting that history is contested.** Real history has competing narratives. Your in-world history should too. Different cultures remember the same war differently.
- **Excess precision.** Knowing the exact year of every event of three thousand years suggests the author has perfect omniscience, which strips out the *texture of lost knowledge* that makes history feel real.

## Patterns from real history

`references/worldbuilder/precedents.md` sections 6 and 13 hold the big shapes (systemic collapse, plague and labor, slow climate change, contact and exchange) and the classic theories of historical change (Ibn Khaldun's cohesion cycle, the dynastic cycle, Polybius, Turchin, Braudel). Use them as lenses for an era's texture, and check section 14 before writing daily life, so the past does not repeat popular myths.

## Originality moves

- **Build the silence.** The most evocative history has visible gaps. The Lost Century. The Years That Aren't Written About. The Sealed Archives.
- **Invert the arrow.** Most fantasy histories are decline-from-golden-age or progress-toward-now. Try a history where the present is genuinely middling — better than some pasts, worse than others, with no overall arc.
- **Make the historians characters.** Different in-world chroniclers wrote with different agendas. Quote them in your world bible; let them disagree.
- **Tie history to landscape.** Every battle leaves a place name. Every massacre leaves a haunted hill. Every saint leaves a shrine. Layer history into geography until walking through the world is walking through time.
- **Use the long now.** Premodern people often had a vastly different sense of time — generational, agricultural, mythic. Render a chronology in *farming generations* or *king-lifespans* or *eclipses* rather than abstract years.

## Output format

```
# History

## Calendar & dating
What year-zero is. How time is counted in-world.

## Era summary
Named eras with one-paragraph flavor each.

## [Era 1 name]
### Themes & feel
What life was like; what people thought was happening.
### Key events
Bulleted, dated where useful.

## [Era 2 name]
...

## Active grievances & memory
What past events still drive present politics.

## Lost knowledge
What the present doesn't know that the past did.

## Open questions
```

For sprawling histories, split into separate files per era: `history/01-first-age.md`, `history/02-twilight.md`, etc.

## Simulated history

When the user wants history generated rather than authored, or wants to know where the story is in the history they have, run saga (`modules/saga.md`). Its `to_bible.py` drafts this file's format from a chronicle (eras cut at turning points, dated key events, genealogies, grievances, suppressed history), with every line marked proposed. The user strikes and renames; nothing in the draft is canon until they ratify it.
