# Religions & Mythology

A religion is not a list of gods. It's a system of meaning that answers what life is for, what death is, what the world is, and what people owe each other. Build the meaning first; the pantheon falls out of it.

## The metaphysical question (answer this first)

Before anything else, decide: **how real is the religion in this world?**

- **True religion** — gods exist, intervene, can be spoken to. Faith is verified.
- **Real but absent** — gods exist or existed, but don't intervene. Faith is inferred from evidence.
- **Real but misunderstood** — something is out there, but the theology has it wrong.
- **False but functional** — no gods, but the institution still does political/social work.
- **Multiple compete and at least one is true** — the most fertile for drama.
- **Ambiguous** — even the author/GM may not know. Highest mystery, lowest legibility.

This single choice changes everything downstream. A world where prayer reliably works is fundamentally different from one where it might.

## The seven core questions

Every religion must take a position on these. The positions don't have to be consistent — most real religions aren't.

1. **Cosmology.** How did the world begin? How will it end? What lies beyond?
2. **Anthropology.** What is a person? What's the soul made of? Why do humans exist?
3. **Ethics.** What is good? What is sin? Who decides?
4. **Soteriology.** What happens after death? Can it be influenced? By whom?
5. **Authority.** Who speaks for the divine? Priests, prophets, texts, the king, no one?
6. **Practice.** What do believers actually do — daily, weekly, yearly, lifelong?
7. **The other.** What do they think about unbelievers, other faiths, heretics?

## Pantheons (if applicable)

If polytheistic, decide the *structure* before naming gods:

- **Functional pantheon** — gods of distinct domains (war, harvest, love). Clean but predictable.
- **Familial pantheon** — gods as a family with relationships and grudges. Drama-rich.
- **Hierarchical** — one supreme god, layered subordinates. Tends toward henotheism.
- **Bickering peers** — no hierarchy; politics among gods. Mirrors human politics.
- **Aspects of one** — many gods are faces of a single underlying truth. Mystical, often elite theology layered over folk polytheism.
- **Local/animist** — every river, tree, ancestor has a spirit. No central pantheon.

Avoid "god of X" as a complete characterization. A god of war who also loves children and writes poetry is more interesting than one defined by their domain. Real gods (Athena, Loki, Hanuman, Kali) are full characters.

## Monotheisms

If monotheistic, the questions get harder, not easier:

- Why does evil exist if the god is good and powerful? (the theodicy problem — every monotheism has an answer; what's this one's?)
- What's the god's personality? (Old Testament Yahweh, the Quranic Allah, Christian Trinity, deist clockmaker — radically different)
- How does the god communicate? Prophets, scripture, mystics, inner voice, never?
- What's the relationship between the god and the natural world? Identical, separate, layered?

## Folk vs. official religion

**Always have both.** Every real religion has the version the priests teach and the version people actually practice — household shrines, lucky charms, syncretic festivals, saint-equivalents, dead relatives consulted on big decisions. The gap between them is rich territory:

- Peasants in a strict monotheism still leaving offerings to old gods at the crossroads
- Elite mystery cults inside a popular state religion
- A "harmless" folk practice the priesthood is quietly furious about

## Institutions

Religions usually have institutions, and institutions have problems. Think about:

- **Hierarchy:** Who's at the top? How are they chosen? How do they fall?
- **Wealth & land:** Most premodern faiths owned tremendous property. Where does the money come from and go to?
- **Schisms:** What's the religion fought about? Every major faith has its theological civil wars.
- **Heresies:** What positions are forbidden? What got someone burned? (Heresies are often more interesting than orthodoxy.)
- **Reformers:** Is there a movement to return to "true" practice? Against what?
- **The dispossessed:** Who's left out — by gender, class, region, profession — and what do they do about it?

## Ritual & practice

A religion without weekly texture isn't a religion, it's a worldview. Specify:

- **Daily:** Prayer? Greeting formula? Dietary rule? Direction faced?
- **Weekly/cyclical:** Sabbath? Market-day rite? Lunar cycle observance?
- **Annual:** Major festivals — what do people actually do? Food, dress, songs, taboos.
- **Lifecycle:** Birth, naming, coming of age, marriage, death — each one a ceremony.
- **Crisis:** What do people do when ill, bereaved, in love, before battle?
- **Spaces:** Temples, shrines, household altars, sacred geography, pilgrimage sites.

Specific is good. A festival where families share a meal of bitter herbs and the youngest child asks four ritual questions is more vivid than "an annual feast of remembrance."

## Originality moves

- **Make the god morally complex.** Even monotheist gods can have aspects believers are uncomfortable with. (The god of mercy is also the god of plague.)
- **Make practice contradict belief.** Believers say one thing; their rituals encode another. (A faith that preaches equality but seats people by rank at services.)
- **Localize the cosmology.** Most fantasy religions have generic afterlives. What's specifically *strange* about this one's heaven, hell, rebirth, oblivion?
- **Make the priesthood mortal.** Priests with families, debts, doubts, ambitions are more interesting than robed figures.
- **Religion of the conquered.** A faith that survives an empire's collapse, twisted by exile, is more textured than a state religion.
- **A religion built on a misunderstanding.** The "miracle" was a comet, an autistic child's gift, a clever fraud — and the faith is still real because of what it does for people.

## Mythology

Myths are stories the religion tells about itself. They don't have to be theologically consistent — real mythologies almost never are. Useful categories:

- **Origin myth** — how the world / the people came to be
- **Hero cycle** — exemplary figure who established the order
- **Trickster cycle** — figure who breaks the order; often the most beloved
- **Catastrophe** — what went wrong, what we lost
- **Eschatology** — what's coming, how it ends

Myths often preserve memory of historical events (floods, migrations, plagues, eclipses) in symbolic form. Embedding real history in myth is a powerful technique.

## Common pitfalls

- **Pantheon-as-roster.** Listing 12 gods with one-line domains is character-sheet worldbuilding, not religion-building.
- **Strawman religion.** Religions designed only to be wrong/oppressive feel cheap. Even the worst real religions retain because they answer real human needs. Honor that.
- **Forgetting the mundane.** Theology is one layer. What does Tuesday look like for a believer?
- **No internal diversity.** Real faiths contain liberals and fundamentalists, mystics and lawyers, sincere believers and cynics. So should fictional ones.
- **One-to-one Earth maps.** "These are the fantasy Catholics, these are the fantasy Vikings." Borrow elements; don't transplant the whole package. Recombine.

## Output format

```
# [Religion name]

## Core claim
One paragraph: what this faith says is true about reality.

## Cosmology & afterlife
Origin, structure of the world, what happens after death.

## Pantheon / divine figures
With personality, not just domain.

## Ethics
What's righteous, what's sin, how it's enforced.

## Practice
Daily, weekly, annual, lifecycle. Specific rituals.

## Institutions
Hierarchy, priesthood, wealth, sacred sites.

## Tensions & schisms
What this faith fights about, internally and externally.

## Folk layer
What people actually do, vs. what doctrine says.

## Mythology
Key stories. (Optionally as separate file if long.)

## Open questions
```
