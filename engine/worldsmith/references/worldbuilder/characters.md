# Characters

Characters anchor a world. A reader/player remembers people, not systems. A world without specific, well-built people in it is set-dressing, no matter how detailed the lore.

## The two kinds of characters in worldbuilding

1. **Story characters** — PCs, protagonists, antagonists, major NPCs. Built deeply, with arc and interiority.
2. **World characters** — figures who populate the lore: historical kings, founding saints, current power-holders the heroes haven't met. Built broadly, with role and shape.

Both matter; don't conflate them. A world character doesn't need a childhood trauma; a story character does.

## The minimum viable character

For *any* character worth writing down:

- **Name** — that suits the culture (see `references/worldbuilder/languages.md`)
- **Role** — what they do, formal and actual
- **Want** — what they're trying to get (concrete and short-term)
- **Need** — what they actually require, often invisible to themselves
- **Wound** — what made them this way
- **Voice** — one line that demonstrates how they talk

That's enough to bring almost any character to life. Expand from there.

## The wider character anatomy

When building a major character, work through:

### Surface
- Name, age, appearance, voice, mannerism
- Where they live, what they wear, what they carry
- First impression vs. true nature

### Position
- Where they sit in society — class, profession, family
- Who has authority over them, who they have authority over
- Their resources: money, allies, debts, reputation, leverage

### Interior
- **Want** (the concrete goal driving them right now)
- **Need** (the deeper thing they're missing or avoiding)
- **Wound** (the past event that shaped this)
- **Fear** (what they're protecting themselves from)
- **Virtue** (the best thing about them, often hidden)
- **Flaw** (the trait that will cost them; often the shadow of the virtue)
- **Self-image vs. reality** (the gap is character)

### Relationships
- Family (loved, hated, dead, complicated)
- Friends and enemies (real ones, not generic)
- Rivals (who do they measure themselves against?)
- Romantic / sexual history (if relevant)
- Mentors and mentees, debts and grudges

### Behavior in pressure
- What do they do under stress? Threat? Loss? Praise? Boredom?
- What's their worst impulse?
- What's their best instinct?
- What would make them break a deeply held rule?

### Arc potential
- What lie do they believe about themselves or the world?
- What event might confront them with the truth?
- What would they have to lose to grow?

## Voice

The single highest-leverage tool. A character with a distinct voice feels real on the page even when the rest is thin. Two techniques:

- **Vocabulary range.** Does this character use long sentences or short? Latinate or Anglo-Saxon roots? Technical jargon or folk wisdom? Casual or formal register?
- **Quirks of speech.** A verbal tic, a favored metaphor source (everything's a sailing reference; everything's a kitchen reference), a refusal (won't curse; won't use someone's title), a specific grammatical pattern.

Write a single line of dialogue that *only* this character would say. If anyone else could plausibly have said it, the voice isn't there yet.

## Names

Don't underestimate naming. A name should:

- Fit the culture (per `references/worldbuilder/languages.md` naming conventions)
- Sound like the person — short and sharp for a soldier; rolling and ornate for a noble; with a folk softness for a peasant
- Avoid accidental real-world echoes (proper noun searches before committing)
- Be pronounceable, unless deliberately not (and apostrophes alone don't make a name alien — see `references/worldbuilder/languages.md`)

Bynames, titles, and forms of address often do more work than the personal name. *Bren of the Long Road* tells you more than *Brendarius*.

## Character and culture

A character is partly the *unique* in them and partly the *typical*. Build both:

- What's typical of their culture, class, profession? (See `references/worldbuilder/cultures.md`)
- What's unusual? Where does this character deviate from the cultural norm, and why?
- How does the culture treat someone like them? Honored, dismissed, feared?

A character whose every trait is "their culture" is a stereotype. A character with no cultural grounding floats free of the world. The interplay is where they come alive.

## Antagonists

Particular notes:

- **Make them right about something.** The best antagonists have a genuine point. Even if their methods are monstrous, the diagnosis is partially correct. This gives the protagonist something to actually wrestle with.
- **Make them coherent.** Antagonists who do evil "because evil" are inert. Specify the worldview that makes their actions feel justified to them.
- **Give them a virtue.** A loyalty, a love, a kept promise, an aesthetic line they won't cross. Pure evil is less terrifying than partial humanity.
- **Make them mortal.** Vulnerable. Capable of fear, doubt, fatigue. An invincible antagonist is a puzzle, not a person.

A useful exercise: write a paragraph of the story from the antagonist's POV. If you can't, you don't know them well enough.

## Minor characters

A world needs minor characters — the innkeeper, the courier, the guard at the gate — who appear briefly but feel real. Give each one:

- A name (not "the innkeeper")
- A *single* specifying detail (he keeps the lantern wicks trimmed himself; she always answers questions with another question; he flinches at thunder)
- A relationship to one of the major characters or to the local situation

Three sentences of work per minor character pays off enormously.

## Historical / legendary characters

For figures who exist only in backstory:

- What did they *actually* do?
- What are they *remembered* for doing?
- What is *legend* about them, possibly false?
- Who remembers them positively? Negatively? Why?
- What present-day institution, place name, or grievance traces back to them?

The gap between truth, memory, and legend is often more interesting than any of them alone.

## Common pitfalls

- **The trauma-as-personality character.** A backstory wound is one piece; it shouldn't be the whole person. Give them present-tense desires, irritations, hobbies, jokes.
- **The skill-tree character.** Defined entirely by capabilities ("master swordsman, polyglot, healer"). What do they *want* and *fear*?
- **The exposition delivery vehicle.** Characters who exist to explain the world to the reader. Better: characters who have lives the world happens to be visible through.
- **Same voice on all characters.** Most fictional dialogue has one voice — the author's. Push for actual range.
- **Cool-by-fiat antiheroes.** The "morally grey" character who never actually does anything morally grey. Make the cost real, or don't claim it.
- **Female / queer / disabled / minority characters defined by that single trait.** That trait is part of them; it isn't them. The same anatomy applies as to any character.

## Originality moves

- **Make the want banal.** Worldview-historical-mythic characters whose want is "to be respected by my mother" or "to retire to a vineyard" become instantly more real.
- **Specify the body.** Most fiction's characters have generic bodies. Specify how they move, their breathing, their hands, what hurts when they're tired.
- **Give them an aesthetic.** Characters with taste — in music, food, art, color — feel like people. Even a peasant has an opinion on what good bread is.
- **Let them be wrong.** Characters whose judgment the narrative endorses get boring. Give them blind spots the reader can see around.
- **Cross-category characters.** The pious thief. The illiterate scholar. The cowardly hero. The cheerful undertaker. Combine traits that "shouldn't" go together and a person emerges.

## Output format

For a story character:

```
# [Character name]

## In a line
One sentence that captures them.

## Surface
Age, appearance, voice, dress, mannerism.

## Position
Role, class, resources, authority.

## Interior
Want / need / wound / fear / virtue / flaw / self-image gap.

## Relationships
Family, friends, enemies, rivals, lovers, debts.

## Under pressure
What they do when threatened, lost, praised, bored.

## Arc potential
The lie they believe; what could confront them with truth.

## Voice
One characteristic line of dialogue.

## Open questions
```

For world characters (historical, legendary, distant), simplify:

```
# [Name]

## Role / claim to attention

## Actual deeds

## Remembered for

## Legend / mythology around them

## Present-day residue
What still traces back to them.
```
