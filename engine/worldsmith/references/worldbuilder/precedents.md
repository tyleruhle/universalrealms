# Precedents: how real societies actually did it

The historian's library. Each entry gives a real case, the **mechanism** that
made it work, and a **transform** prompt for turning the mechanism into
something new. Borrow the mechanism; change the surface. A precedent cited in
a sentence gives an invention weight; a precedent transplanted whole makes a
world feel like a costume party.

## 1. Using precedent well

- **Mechanism over costume.** The question is always "why did this work, for
  whom, at what cost?" The answer transfers; the helmets do not.
- **Mix sources.** One society built from three unrelated precedents feels
  original. One society built from one precedent feels borrowed.
- **Keep the friction.** Real institutions had losers, loopholes and
  exceptions. Keep them; they are where stories live.
- **Be honest about certainty.** Where historians disagree, say "contested"
  or "often credited." Never invent a citation, date or quotation. If unsure
  of a detail, describe the pattern without the detail.
- **Light touch in conversation.** A phrase ("as the Venetians did") is
  usually enough. Go deeper only when the user is curious.

## 2. Power, legitimacy and succession

**Tanistry (Gaelic Ireland and Scotland).** The successor (tánaiste) was
chosen from a kin group descending from a common great-grandfather, often
during the ruler's lifetime. *Mechanism:* a wide pool of eligible heirs means
competence can win, and so can violence. *Transform:* a merchant house where
any great-grandchild may stand for the chair, and cousins campaign for years.

**Ottoman succession.** Mehmed II's law code sanctioned a new sultan executing
his brothers to prevent civil war; from the early seventeenth century this gave
way to confining princes in the palace (the *kafes*) and passing the throne to
the eldest male of the dynasty. *Mechanism:* stability bought with fratricide,
then with princes raised in isolation and unfit to rule. *Transform:* heirs
are stored in cryosleep until needed and wake decades out of date.

**Born in the purple (Byzantium).** *Porphyrogennetos* marked a child born to
a reigning emperor in the palace's porphyry chamber, a legitimacy claim over
siblings born before their father took the throne. *Mechanism:* legitimacy
attached to the circumstance of birth, not birth order. *Transform:* only
children born during a comet's visibility can inherit.

**The kurultai (Mongol Empire).** A great council of princes and commanders
confirmed a new khan. Distances made convening slow, and the Mongol withdrawal
from Central Europe in 1242 is often credited to the need to return after
Ögedei's death (historians debate how much). *Mechanism:* succession requires
physical assembly, so geography shapes politics. *Transform:* an interstellar
empire whose emperor is chosen by delegates who need forty years to arrive.

**The Venetian doge.** From 1268 the doge was chosen by alternating rounds of
lot and election through a chain of shrinking and growing committees.
*Mechanism:* randomness defeats faction-building. *Transform:* see the ship
captaincy example in `references/worldbuilder/spark.md`.

**Elective monarchy and the liberum veto (Poland-Lithuania).** Nobles elected
the king, and any single deputy could dissolve the Sejm with a veto.
*Mechanism:* protection of minority rights pushed to paralysis, making the
state easy for neighbors to manipulate. *Transform:* a council where any
member may halt all business by fasting in the chamber.

**Dual kingship (Sparta).** Two kings from two royal houses ruled together,
alongside elected ephors. *Mechanism:* built-in rivalry as a check.
*Transform:* two hereditary rulers, one for the land and one for the dead.

**Slave-soldier rulers (the Mamluks of Egypt, 1250 to 1517).** Military
slaves, purchased and trained, became the ruling elite; their sons were often
excluded from the core of power. *Mechanism:* an elite renewed from outside
each generation, loyal to the institution rather than to blood. *Transform:*
an order whose members must be adopted and whose biological children are
barred.

**The examination state (imperial China).** Civil service exams, developing
from the Sui and maturing under the Song, selected officials by learning until
their abolition in 1905. *Mechanism:* legitimacy through mastery of a canon;
education as the path to power. *Transform:* officials chosen by a test of
memory, so the archive's contents shape who governs.

**The Mandate of Heaven (China).** Rulers governed by heaven's mandate, which
disasters, famine and rebellion could show to have been withdrawn.
*Mechanism:* legitimacy that is revocable and readable in events. *Transform:*
any ruler whose harvests fail three years running can be lawfully challenged.

**The Great Law of Peace (Haudenosaunee).** In the confederacy, clan mothers
nominated chiefs and could remove them. *Mechanism:* the power to select and
depose held by a different group than the power to govern. *Transform:*
generals are appointed and recalled by a guild of widows.

**The Golden Stool (Asante).** A sacred stool held to house the spirit of the
nation; no one sat on it, and the British governor's demand for it in 1900
sparked war. *Mechanism:* legitimacy embodied in an object that outranks any
person. *Transform:* a crown that is a living organism and must consent.

## 3. Law, justice and order

**The Althing (Iceland, from 930).** An open-air assembly with a lawspeaker
who recited the law from memory, a third of it each year. *Mechanism:* law
held in a person, not a book. *Transform:* law sung, so the best singers are
the judges and tone-deafness is a legal disability.

**Wergild (early medieval Europe).** A price set on each person by status,
paid to the victim's kin to end a feud. *Mechanism:* violence converted to
debt; social rank becomes an exact number. *Transform:* compensation paid in
years of service, so murderers become the servants of their victim's family.

**Trial by ordeal and combat.** Guilt tested by hot iron, water or a duel,
believed to reveal divine judgment; the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) forbade
clergy to take part in ordeals, which hastened their decline. *Mechanism:*
proof outsourced to the sacred. *Transform:* guilt decided by whether a
witness animal will eat from the accused's hand.

**Ostracism (Athens).** Citizens scratched a name on pottery shards; the
"winner" went into exile for ten years without loss of property. *Mechanism:*
a pressure valve against would-be tyrants. *Transform:* an annual vote to
freeze one citizen in stasis for a decade.

**Pashtunwali.** A code including hospitality to any guest (*melmastia*) and
the obligation of revenge (*badal*). *Mechanism:* honor obligations that can
conflict, so a host may have to shelter his enemy. *Transform:* sanctuary laws
that bind even when the guest is the host's family's killer.

## 4. Money, trade and economy

**Rai stones (Yap).** Large carved stone discs; ownership could pass without
moving them, and one famously sank at sea yet still counted as wealth.
*Mechanism:* money as shared memory. *Transform:* currency that exists only as
a song everyone in a town knows.

**The kula ring (Trobriand Islands).** Shell necklaces and armbands circulated
in opposite directions around an island ring, never kept, gaining prestige
with each holder (described by Malinowski). *Mechanism:* value from
circulation and history, not use. *Transform:* heirloom weapons that must be
passed on within a year or bring disgrace.

**Potlatch (Pacific Northwest).** Feasts where hosts gave away or destroyed
wealth to gain status; Canada banned the practice from 1885 to 1951.
*Mechanism:* status by giving. *Transform:* a nobility ranked by how much they
have publicly destroyed.

**The Hanseatic League.** A network of merchant towns with foreign trading
posts (*Kontore*) at London, Bruges, Bergen and Novgorod, able to embargo
whole kingdoms. *Mechanism:* economic power without a state. *Transform:* a
guild of ferrymen that can blockade every river crossing on a continent.

**The Venetian Arsenal.** State shipyards that built galleys in a sequence of
specialized stations, able to produce ships at remarkable speed. *Mechanism:*
industrial organization centuries early. *Transform:* a magic that can only be
produced by a line of specialists, each knowing one step.

**Mansa Musa's pilgrimage (1324).** The Mali emperor's gold spending in Cairo
is reported to have depressed the gold price there for years. *Mechanism:*
one actor's wealth distorts a foreign economy. *Transform:* a pilgrim queen
whose alms collapse the value of a holy city's coin.

**Tally sticks (England).** Notched wooden sticks, split in two, recorded
debts to the Exchequer for centuries; burning old tallies in 1834 started the
fire that destroyed the Palace of Westminster. *Mechanism:* a record you
cannot forge alone. *Transform:* contracts grown as two halves of one tree.

**Hawala.** An informal trust network moving value across distance without
moving money, settled between brokers. *Mechanism:* trust as infrastructure.
*Transform:* a network of letter-sealers whose word moves fortunes across war
lines.

**Spice monopolies (Banda Islands).** Nutmeg's only source drew the Dutch
East India Company into conquest; the Treaty of Breda (1667) confirmed the
Dutch hold on the island of Run and English possession of New Netherland,
including Manhattan. *Mechanism:* a single source makes a speck of land worth
a war. *Transform:* the only world where a navigation crystal forms.

## 5. Belief, schism and the sacred

**The Arian controversy and Nicaea (325).** A dispute over whether the Son was
of the same substance (*homoousios*) as the Father or of similar substance
(*homoiousios*): one letter apart, fought over for generations. *Mechanism:*
tiny doctrinal differences become political identity. *Transform:* a schism
over whether a god's name has two syllables or three.

**The Great Schism (1054).** Latin and Greek churches split over authority
and the *filioque* clause, among other issues. *Mechanism:* a long drift made
final by one confrontation. *Transform:* a faith split over which direction
to face in prayer once its people spread to a second planet.

**Akhenaten's Aten cult.** An Egyptian pharaoh elevated one solar deity and
built a new capital; his successors abandoned both and erased his name.
*Mechanism:* top-down religious revolution, then deliberate forgetting.
*Transform:* a reign removed from every record except the enemy's.

**The Taiping Rebellion (1850 to 1864).** Led by Hong Xiuquan, who believed
himself the younger brother of Jesus; among the deadliest conflicts of the
nineteenth century. *Mechanism:* a visionary synthesis meeting a failing
state. *Transform:* a prophet who reads a foreign holy text and finds himself
in it.

**Cargo cults (Melanesia).** Movements such as the John Frum movement on
Tanna (Vanuatu) linked ritual to the arrival of goods seen during colonial
contact and war. *Mechanism:* new religion explains sudden, unexplained
abundance. *Transform:* a colony that worships the long-silent supply drops.

**Syncretism (Santería, Candomblé).** West African orishas maintained through
correspondence with Catholic saints. *Mechanism:* a suppressed faith survives
inside the dominant one. *Transform:* forbidden gods hidden in the
iconography of the state religion.

**The Cathars and the Albigensian Crusade.** A dualist movement in southern
France crushed by crusade and inquisition in the thirteenth century.
*Mechanism:* heresy as regional identity, crushed by a northern power with
its own interests. *Transform:* a heresy that is really a province's
independence movement.

**Towers of Silence (Zoroastrian).** The dead exposed to birds so as not to
pollute earth or fire. *Mechanism:* purity rules shape architecture and
ecology. *Transform:* a culture whose dead are given to the sea's tides, so
the coast is sacred and fishing is complicated.

## 6. The big shapes of history

**Late Bronze Age collapse (around 1200 to 1150 BCE).** Interconnected palace
states of the eastern Mediterranean fell within decades; proposed causes
include drought, earthquakes, raiders (the "Sea Peoples"), and the fragility
of the trade system itself. *Mechanism:* systemic collapse when a network's
links fail together. *Transform:* a trade league of worlds that falls when its
one gate fails.

**The Black Death (1347 to 1351).** Killed perhaps a third to a half of
Europe; labor shortages raised wages, prompted laws to cap them (the Statute
of Labourers, 1351), and fed unrest such as the Peasants' Revolt of 1381.
*Mechanism:* catastrophe that shifts bargaining power to survivors.
*Transform:* a plague that kills only the literate.

**The Little Ice Age.** A cooler period roughly from the fourteenth to the
nineteenth century; the Norse Greenland settlements were abandoned during the
fifteenth century, with climate one of several factors. *Mechanism:* slow
change that no single generation sees. *Transform:* a colony that does not
notice its world's star dimming until the fields fail.

**The Year Without a Summer (1816).** After Tambora's 1815 eruption, crops
failed across the Northern Hemisphere. *Mechanism:* a distant event, an
immediate local famine. *Transform:* an eruption on another continent read as
divine judgment on a local king.

**The Columbian Exchange.** Plants, animals and diseases crossed the Atlantic:
potatoes transformed European diets, horses transformed the Plains nations,
epidemics devastated the Americas. *Mechanism:* contact reshapes both sides
in unexpected ways. *Transform:* first contact where the visitors' crops do
more than their weapons.

**The Peace of Westphalia (1648).** Ended the Thirty Years' War and is often
treated as a milestone in the idea of sovereign states. *Mechanism:*
exhaustion produces new rules of the game. *Transform:* a war so ruinous that
its peace treaty becomes the constitution of a civilization.

**Classic Maya decline (around the ninth century).** Many southern lowland
cities were abandoned; drought, warfare and environmental strain are leading
explanations. *Mechanism:* cities emptied while people and culture continued
elsewhere. *Transform:* ruins whose descendants live nearby and refuse to
explain them.

## 7. War and logistics

**Logistics decides.** Armies were limited by what they could carry or
forage; Roman legions built camps nightly and roads for supply; the Mongols
moved with multiple remounts per rider. *Mechanism:* reach is bounded by food,
fodder and water. *Transform:* a war where the binding constraint is a
fungus soldiers must eat daily that only grows at home.

**Fall of Constantinople (1453).** Ottoman siege artillery, including great
bombards associated with the founder Orban, breached walls that had stood for
a millennium. *Mechanism:* one technology ends a strategic certainty.
*Transform:* the invention that makes the impregnable city's walls a museum.

**Pike squares (Swiss).** Disciplined infantry with pikes defeated mounted
knights in the fifteenth century. *Mechanism:* organization beats elite
individuals. *Transform:* peasant militias trained to break the ritual duels
of a warrior caste.

## 8. Knowledge, technology and what gets lost

**Roman concrete.** Volcanic ash mixtures produced harbor structures that
have lasted two thousand years; researchers have been reconstructing why.
*Mechanism:* a technique that outlasts the understanding of it.
*Transform:* ancient roads that repair themselves, and no one knows how.

**The Antikythera mechanism.** A geared astronomical calculator from around
the second or first century BCE, without known equal for centuries.
*Mechanism:* sophistication that left no tradition. *Transform:* a single
recovered device that proves the ancients could predict the eclipses the
priests claim as miracles.

**Greek fire and wootz steel.** Byzantine incendiary weapons and the crucible
steel behind "Damascus" blades both had techniques that were later lost.
*Mechanism:* secrets held by small groups die with them. *Transform:* a
weapon whose last maker is a prisoner of the enemy.

**Khipu (Inca).** Knotted cords recorded numbers and possibly narrative; the
Inca road system ran on relay runners (*chasqui*). *Mechanism:* a sophisticated
state without alphabetic writing. *Transform:* an empire that records history
in braided hair.

**Movable type (Bi Sheng, 1040s) and the House of Wisdom (Baghdad).**
Innovation and translation centers that shaped how knowledge spread.
*Mechanism:* institutions, not geniuses, carry knowledge forward.
*Transform:* a translation house that is the most powerful political body in
the empire.

## 9. Land, water and ecology

**Qanats (Persia).** Gently sloping underground channels bringing groundwater
from hills to plains, maintained over generations. *Mechanism:* water
infrastructure as inheritance and as power. *Transform:* the families who
know the tunnels' routes are the true nobility.

**Rice terraces (Ifugao, Philippines).** Mountain terraces sustained by
shared water management. *Mechanism:* landscape as a multi-generational
collective artifact. *Transform:* a terraced world whose terraces fail if one
village stops its upkeep.

**Polynesian wayfinding.** Navigation by stars, swells and birds across the
Pacific; Marshall Islands stick charts mapped swell patterns; the Hōkūleʻa's
1976 voyage to Tahiti helped revive traditional navigation. *Mechanism:*
knowledge held in practice and memory. *Transform:* starship pilots who read
gravitational "swells" and refuse to use instruments.

**Monsoon trade.** Seasonal winds set the calendar of Indian Ocean commerce.
*Mechanism:* climate as timetable. *Transform:* a world where trade is
possible only in the months a tidal bridge is exposed.

**Introduced species.** Rabbits in Australia became a continental ecological
disaster. *Mechanism:* a small introduction, an exponential problem.
*Transform:* a pet brought by settlers that eats the native magic.

**The Aral Sea.** Diversion of feeder rivers for irrigation shrank one of the
world's largest lakes. *Mechanism:* engineering success in one place, ruin in
another. *Transform:* a kingdom's great dam that dried its neighbor's holy
lake.

## 10. Language, writing and memory

**The Great Vowel Shift (English, roughly 1400 to 1700).** Long vowels moved
while spelling largely stayed. *Mechanism:* speech changes faster than
writing, leaving fossil spellings. *Transform:* sacred texts whose words are
pronounced in a way no living person understands.

**Linear B and Linear A.** Linear B was deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris
as an early form of Greek; Linear A remains undeciphered. *Mechanism:* a
script can be read without the culture being understood, or not read at all.
*Transform:* an archive that is legible only to one heretic.

**Dead languages that live on.** Sumerian was copied by scribes long after it
ceased to be spoken, as Latin later became a church and scholarly language.
*Mechanism:* prestige languages outlive their speakers. *Transform:* the
language of the extinct founders as the only legal language for contracts.

**Prestige registers.** Persian at the Mughal court; French among the Russian
aristocracy. *Mechanism:* the elite speaks a language the peasants do not.
*Transform:* nobles who speak only in a machine language.

**Taboo replacement.** The common Germanic word for "bear" is thought to come
from a descriptive "the brown one," replacing an older name, possibly from
taboo. *Mechanism:* fear changes vocabulary. *Transform:* a creature whose
real name everyone has forgotten, because saying it summons it.

**Whistled language (Silbo Gomero).** A whistled form of Spanish used across
ravines on La Gomera. *Mechanism:* terrain shapes the medium of speech.
*Transform:* a mountain people whose speech carries across valleys and cannot
be whispered.

## 11. Custom and daily life

**Xenia (ancient Greece).** Sacred guest-friendship binding host and guest,
with obligations passed down families. *Mechanism:* hospitality as treaty.
*Transform:* two warring houses bound to shelter each other's travelers.

**Sakoku (Japan, 1630s to 1850s).** Severe restrictions on foreign contact,
with limited trade through Nagasaki. *Mechanism:* a controlled aperture to
the world. *Transform:* a planet that permits one ship a year, and whoever
crews it holds enormous power.

**Market and feast calendars.** Agricultural societies ran on festivals tied
to sowing, harvest and slaughter. *Mechanism:* time is felt through work.
*Transform:* a calendar counted in harvests on a world with irregular
seasons.

## 12. Real science for science fiction

- **Light lag.** Signals between Earth and Mars take roughly 3 to 22 minutes
  depending on orbital positions. Interstellar governance without
  faster-than-light messaging means colonies are effectively sovereign.
- **Tidally locked worlds.** One face always to the star; life clusters in
  the terminator band, with permanent day and night on either side.
- **The Kardashev scale.** Civilizations ranked by energy use at planetary,
  stellar and galactic scale. Useful for calibrating powers.
- **O'Neill cylinders and Dyson swarms.** Rotating habitats with spin gravity;
  swarms of collectors around a star. Each implies an economy of maintenance.
- **Generation ships.** Social engineering over centuries in a closed system:
  succession, ideology drift, and the question of what the mission means to
  the grandchildren.
- **Solar storms.** The Carrington Event (1859) induced currents in telegraph
  lines; a modern equivalent would threaten power grids. A natural disaster
  only an electrical civilization fears.
- **The Fermi paradox.** If others exist, why the silence? Every answer is a
  setting premise.

## 13. Theories of history (and how they map to saga)

These give the historian's voice a frame and connect directly to the saga
simulator's gauges (`references/saga/simulation.md`).

- **Ibn Khaldun's *asabiyyah* (fourteenth century).** Group solidarity lets a
  frontier people conquer; settled luxury erodes it over a few generations,
  and a new cohesive group takes over. In saga terms this is **cohesion**:
  high for new factions, eroded by size and comfort.
- **Polybius's *anacyclosis*.** Constitutions cycle: monarchy, tyranny,
  aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob rule, back to monarchy. A frame for
  how a faction's government type might change over eras.
- **Turchin's structural-demographic theory.** Population growth and elite
  overproduction (too many aspirants for too few positions) raise instability;
  crises cluster in cycles. In saga terms: contested successions and claimant
  wars should rise when many heirs and claims accumulate.
- **Braudel's *longue durée*.** Geography and climate change slowly beneath
  the fast churn of events. In worldsmith terms: worldforge is the long
  duration, saga is the churn.
- **The dynastic cycle (Chinese historiography).** Founding vigor, prosperity,
  corruption, disaster read as loss of the Mandate, rebellion, new dynasty. In
  saga terms: **legitimacy** falling through disasters and lost wars until a
  rebellion or claimant succeeds.

Use them as lenses for explaining a simulated history, not as laws. Each has
critics, and real history is messier than any cycle.

## 14. Myths about the past that make worlds feel fake

Avoid these unless a culture in the world believes them for a reason:

- **Horned Viking helmets.** No evidence for battle use; the image owes much
  to nineteenth-century costume design, including productions of Wagner.
- **Medieval people thought the Earth was flat.** Educated medieval Europeans
  generally knew it was a sphere.
- **Average life expectancy of thirty means people died at thirty.** Low
  averages were dragged down by infant and child mortality; adults who
  survived childhood often lived into their fifties, sixties or beyond.
- **Heavy, clumsy swords.** Most medieval swords weighed roughly one to one and
  a half kilograms and were well balanced.
- **Full plate armor everywhere.** Full plate harness is mostly a fourteenth-
  and fifteenth-century development; earlier armies relied on mail.
- ***Jus primae noctis.*** There is little or no reliable evidence that a lord's
  "right of the first night" was practiced as law.
- **Chastity belts as common medieval devices.** Largely a later myth.
- **"Dark Ages" with no progress.** The early medieval period saw real
  developments in agriculture, law, scholarship and art; many historians avoid
  the term.
- **Static peasants who never traveled.** Pilgrimage, markets, seasonal labor
  and migration moved people more than the stereotype suggests.
- **Gladiators always fought to the death.** Many bouts ended without a death,
  and the exact "thumbs" gesture for a verdict is uncertain.

Getting these right is a quiet signal of depth. A world whose daily life
avoids them will feel lived-in to readers who know the history, without anyone
noticing why.
