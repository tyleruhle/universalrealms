# Ecology — Flora, Fauna, Monsters

A living world has a living biosphere. Generic "forests" with no specific trees, "wolves" with no specific behavior, and monsters that exist only to be fought are flag-warnings that the ecology layer hasn't been built. Even sketching it lightly adds enormous texture.

## Build the ecosystem, not the bestiary

The temptation is to start with a list of monsters. Resist. Start with **ecosystems** — communities of organisms in particular environments — and let species emerge from them.

For any region (see `references/worldbuilder/geography.md`), specify:

- **Dominant biome:** forest (broadleaf? coniferous? cloud? mangrove?), grassland, desert, marsh, tundra, reef, ocean, subterranean, etc.
- **Producers:** what plants form the base of the food web?
- **Primary consumers:** what eats the plants?
- **Predators:** what eats the herbivores?
- **Top predators:** what (if anything) sits at the top?
- **Decomposers:** what processes the dead?
- **Keystone species:** is there one organism whose presence shapes the whole system? (Real example: beavers create wetlands; remove them, the landscape transforms.)

Even three or four named organisms at each level make a place feel real.

## Plants matter

Fantasy worlds chronically underspecify plants. Plants:

- Feed the herbivores that feed everyone else
- Provide building materials, fuel, medicine, dye, fiber, paper
- Mark seasons (when the bluebells bloom, when the leaves fall)
- Are sacred, taboo, or symbolic in human cultures
- Carry economic weight (cotton, opium, tea, tobacco — single plants reshaped global history)

For each major region, name at least:
- A staple crop or wild food
- A useful wood
- A medicinal / poisonous plant
- A flower or tree with cultural weight

Don't just borrow Earth plants by name unless deliberate. Even renaming them subtly is enough: "ironwood" and "ghostbloom" work as long as they behave consistently.

## Animals

For each region, sketch:

- **Domesticated animals:** what people keep and why (food, draft, fiber, transport, companionship, ritual)
- **Game:** what's hunted
- **Predators:** what threatens livestock and humans
- **Scavengers / parasites:** rats, crows, vultures, leeches — the unglamorous infrastructure of an ecosystem
- **The wonders:** the regionally distinctive species people travel to see

Real-world ecology rule: predators are always far rarer than prey. A landscape full of large predators is unsustainable; build accordingly.

## Monsters & magical creatures

Monsters work best when they have **ecological niches**. A creature that doesn't eat, breed, or interact with its environment isn't an animal — it's a special effect.

For any monster:

- **What does it eat?** (Many fantasy monsters seemingly don't need food. Why not?)
- **What eats it?** (If nothing, why hasn't it overrun the world?)
- **Where does it live?** Specific habitat, not "wilderness."
- **How does it reproduce?** Few young, many young, asexually, by infecting hosts?
- **What's its life cycle?** Larval stage, mating display, migration?
- **What's the population?** Solitary apex, pack, swarm?
- **How does it relate to humans?** Avoided, hunted, worshipped, used, traded?
- **What did people know about it before it appeared in the story?** (Folklore, hunting traditions, magical taxonomy.)

A bullette that lives in a specific desert, breeds during a specific season, has specific signs of presence, is hunted by specific tribes for specific reasons, and is approached using specific traditional methods — that's a creature in a world. A bullette that just shows up to fight the heroes is a stat block.

## The magical layer

If magic exists, it should affect biology. Possibilities:

- **Magic-using species:** creatures that use magic as part of their natural behavior (predators that paralyze with a song; plants that telepathically lure pollinators)
- **Magic-fed species:** organisms that consume magical energy directly, or live in places saturated with it
- **Magic-warped species:** ordinary creatures changed by long exposure to magical environments
- **Symbiotic magic:** species whose biology depends on partnership with a magical entity
- **Magical pests / diseases:** the dark mirror — magic creates new pathologies, parasites, plagues

Each magical species should still answer the basic ecological questions. "Lives off pure mana" is fine if you commit; it implies ecology built around magic-rich areas, fights over those areas, magical seasons.

## Domesticated species

Often overlooked in worldbuilding, despite being central to most human lives. Specify:

- **Beasts of burden:** horses, oxen, mules, camels, yaks — or fantasy analogues
- **Mounts:** different from beasts of burden; bred for speed, war, prestige
- **Food animals:** what's eaten, what isn't (taboos), how prepared
- **Working animals:** dogs, falcons, ferrets, cormorants, cats
- **Vermin co-travelers:** rats, mice, sparrows, lice — the species that follow human settlement uninvited

Domesticated animals tell you a lot about culture. A society without horses fights differently. A society that won't eat pigs lives differently. A society that keeps no dogs hunts differently.

## Disease & parasites

The least-loved ecology topic, but transformative. Real plagues changed history more than most wars. Worldbuilding payoffs:

- **Endemic diseases** that shape where people can live (malaria kept much of Africa underdeveloped; yellow fever doomed European colonization attempts)
- **Crowd diseases** that emerged with cities and changed demographics permanently
- **Plagues in folk memory** that still drive cultural practices (knock on wood, cover the mirrors)
- **Magical diseases** if magic exists — cursed wasting, possession, contagious enchantment

A world without disease feels too clean. Add at least one disease people are afraid of and one custom that exists because of it.

## The wild vs. the cultivated

Where human settlement meets wild ecology is often the most narratively rich zone:

- **Borderlands:** forest edges where wolves take livestock; coast where storms wreck ships; mountains where landslides come
- **Sacred wilds:** places people don't go, by custom or law
- **Cultivated wilds:** managed forests, hunting parks, oyster beds — places that look wild but are shaped
- **Reclaimed land:** drained marsh, terraced hillside, cleared forest — wins against ecology that are also losses

The cleanest "wilderness" in real history is usually shaped by human absence (depopulation by plague, war, displacement). Genuinely untouched wilderness is rarer than fantasy implies.

## Seasonal & cyclical patterns

Ecology happens in time, not just space. For any region:

- **Annual cycle:** when do animals breed, migrate, hibernate? When do plants flower, fruit, drop leaves?
- **Multi-year cycles:** mast years (acorn gluts), locust years, salmon runs of varying intensity
- **Decadal:** cicada emergences, certain fish stocks
- **Longer:** old-growth recovery, soil exhaustion and renewal

A festival timed to the migration. A famine in the years after the locust swarm. A trade good that's only available every seventh year. These details make ecology *eventful*.

## Common pitfalls

- **The kitchen-sink wilderness.** "A forest, with wolves and bears and giants and dragons and elves and..." A real forest has a specific cast.
- **Monsters with no ecology.** Creatures that exist only as combat encounters.
- **The empty wild.** Vast wilderness with no specific named species, only "trees" and "beasts."
- **Carnivore inflation.** Most fantasy maps imply predator densities that no ecology could sustain.
- **Cute fantasy biology.** Plants that only do one thing, animals that exist for one mechanic. Real organisms have layered roles.
- **Domesticated animals as wallpaper.** "Horses" everywhere with no specification of breed, condition, cost, breeding, training, cultural meaning.

## Originality moves

- **Borrow from underused real ecosystems.** Most fantasy is northern temperate forest. Try mangrove, savanna, tepui, sea-cliff, sub-arctic, polar desert.
- **Build the food web from a single weird species.** A keystone plant that feeds a keystone insect that feeds half the ecosystem; remove it and the world collapses.
- **Invent the seasonal calendar.** Twelve named months, each with the animal or plant that marks it. The world organizes around this.
- **Make a creature culturally complex.** Not just "the dragons are evil" but: feared in the south, sacred in the west, hunted in the north, used for transportation in two specific cities. Same species, different relationships.
- **Magic that distorts ecology weirdly.** A region where time runs slow, so a year's growth happens in a season. A grove where dead things don't decompose. A coast where the wind always blows from the same direction, shaping every tree.
- **Plagues with story.** The disease that came with the empire. The blight that killed the staple crop and forced migration. The wasting that takes only firstborns.
