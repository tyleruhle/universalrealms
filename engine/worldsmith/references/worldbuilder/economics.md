# Economics

Economics is the most consistently underbuilt layer of fantasy worlds. It's also where most settings collapse on inspection: cities without food supplies, currencies that make no sense, taverns full of patrons with no apparent trade. Build economics specifically and the world snaps into focus.

## The three foundational questions

For any society:

1. **What feeds the people?** (Agriculture, herding, fishing, hunting, magical means? Where, by whom?)
2. **What do people trade?** (Local goods, regional specialties, long-distance luxuries, services, information?)
3. **How is wealth stored and moved?** (Coin, livestock, land, slaves, debt, letters of credit, magical instruments?)

If these three aren't answered, the economy isn't built yet.

## The base: subsistence

Most premodern societies are 80–95% farmers. This is the bedrock; everyone else lives off the surplus farmers produce. Get this right and the rest follows.

- **Staple crops:** wheat, rice, maize, millet, potatoes, taro — each implies climate, irrigation, calendar, labor pattern
- **Yields:** premodern grain yields were brutally low (5-10x seed sown). One bad year was hunger; two was famine.
- **Livestock:** what's kept, why? Cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, chickens, horses each fit different ecologies and cultures.
- **Hunger:** every premodern society had a hungry season (usually late winter / early spring) before the new harvest. Build it in.

The percentage of population *not* farming is the surplus. A society with 90% farmers can sustain a 10% urban/military/religious class. With 95% farmers, only 5%. Magic or technology that raises productivity changes everything downstream — *but the upstream effect is the population shifting off farms.*

## Trade

Trade exists wherever one place produces what another lacks. For each region (see `references/worldbuilder/geography.md`):

- **Surpluses:** what does it produce in excess?
- **Deficits:** what does it desperately need?
- **Specialties:** what does it produce uniquely or best?

Then ask: how does the trade actually move? Caravans? River barges? Coastal shipping? Magical conveyance? Each has different costs, risks, and seasonality.

**Premodern trade rules of thumb:**
- Water transport is roughly 10-30x cheaper than land transport per ton-mile
- Long-distance overland trade is mostly luxuries (spices, silk, gems, books) because bulk goods don't pay
- Coastal and river towns are wealthier than interior ones, all else equal
- Trade routes carry diseases, ideas, refugees, and spies along with goods

## Money

Money is so common in fiction that its strangeness gets ignored. Specify:

- **What's the currency?** Coin (in what metals, what denominations)? Cowries? Tally sticks? Salt? Cattle? Promises?
- **Who mints it?** State, temple, merchant houses, individual nobles?
- **Is it trusted?** Are coins clipped, debased, counterfeited? Are foreign coins preferred?
- **How is value stored long-term?** Land, livestock, jewelry, art, slaves, books, magical items?
- **How is value moved long-distance?** Carrying coin is risky; letters of credit, bills of exchange, and bank-house networks emerged for this reason.

A useful coin tier (premodern fantasy default):
- **Copper / bronze**: daily transactions; one coin buys a loaf or a small drink
- **Silver**: workman's day-wage range
- **Gold**: substantial purchase; usually weighed rather than spent loose
- **Higher** (platinum, gem, magical): aristocratic / international

If you say a tavern stay costs "a gold piece," what would that be in working-class terms? Often: a week's wages. Adjust accordingly.

## Class & labor

Economic structure makes class structure. Sketch the layers:

- **Slaves / chattel** (if present): how are they acquired, treated, freed if at all?
- **Bound laborers:** serfs, indentured, debt-bound, conscripted
- **Free peasants / smallholders:** owning their own land or tools
- **Artisans:** specialized producers, often guild-organized
- **Merchants:** local, regional, long-distance — the long-distance ones are often wealthier than minor nobility
- **Professionals:** scribes, healers, lawyers, ritualists, teachers
- **Landed elite:** nobility, gentry, large landowners
- **Clergy:** sometimes a parallel hierarchy with its own economy
- **Marginal:** beggars, outlaws, sex workers, wandering performers
- **Foreigners:** often economically vital, often vulnerable to scapegoating

Each layer should have a story: how someone enters it, how someone can rise or fall, what their daily economic life looks like.

## Guilds, monopolies, and economic institutions

In most premodern settings, "free markets" are the exception. Most economic activity is organized:

- **Guilds** regulate who can practice a trade, set prices and standards, train apprentices, support members in hardship
- **Monopolies** are granted by states or held by force
- **Cartels** of merchants control trade in specific goods
- **Religious institutions** often own enormous productive land
- **State monopolies** on critical goods (salt, iron, magical materials) are common revenue sources

A "free merchant" in a guild city often isn't free at all — they belong to a guild that controls every aspect of their work. This is rich texture, not just background.

## Resources

Each major resource in a world implies:

- **Where it's extracted:** mine, forest, sea, magical source
- **Who controls extraction:** state, lord, guild, free workers, slaves
- **How it's processed:** raw goods rarely move to consumers without intermediaries
- **Trade route:** the path from source to user
- **Strategic implications:** wars are fought for resources; pick which ones matter enough to fight over

Critical premodern resources to consider: grain, salt, iron, timber, horses, wool/linen, dyes, spices, precious metals, fish, wine/beer. Magical/sci-fi resources work the same way: where, who, how processed, who fights for them.

## Banking, credit, and finance

By any meaningful level of complexity, societies invent finance. Even premodern systems had:

- **Loans at interest** (sometimes religiously fraught — Christian, Jewish, and Islamic societies handled this differently)
- **Pawnbroking**
- **Bills of exchange** (medieval Italian and Islamic merchants moved enormous sums on paper)
- **Deposit banking** (temples often functioned as banks)
- **Insurance** (especially for shipping)
- **Joint ventures** for risky voyages
- **State debt** (often the lever that broke regimes)

If your world has long-distance trade, it has finance, even if it doesn't call it that. Decide who handles it — bankers, temples, merchant houses, a magical institution.

## When economics breaks the world

Common failure modes that quietly destroy worlds on inspection:

- **Cities too big for their hinterland.** A premodern city of a million needs roughly an empire to feed it (only ancient Rome and a few others managed this).
- **Adventurers with infinite gold sinks.** Tavern-and-inn settings where heroes spend a noble's annual income per week with no economic effect.
- **Heavy industry without infrastructure.** Mass-produced steel weapons without coal/charcoal supply, roads, foundries, organized labor.
- **Magic without market.** Healing magic exists but plagues persist; teleportation exists but trade is by ox-cart; resurrection exists but inheritance still works the normal way. Each of these is a worldbuilding question begging for an answer.
- **Slavery as backdrop.** Treating slavery as a setting detail without thinking through its enormous economic and political consequences. Either build it specifically or don't include it.
- **The everywhere coin.** "Gold pieces" accepted in every shop on every continent. Real currencies were local, often distrusted across borders, and merchants used weight-of-metal calculations as needed.

## Originality moves

- **Pick a strange medium of value.** A society that uses memorized poems as currency. One that uses time. One that uses promises witnessed by a god. Think through implications.
- **Tie the economy to the magic system.** If magic costs something (see `references/worldbuilder/magic-systems.md`), that cost is economic. Mana crystals are oil; spell-components are pharmaceuticals; soul-debt is debt.
- **Build the labor system specifically.** Most fantasy worlds vaguely have "peasants" without specifying tenure, dues, mobility, taxes, and free time. Specify one and watch the world fill in.
- **Make trade routes characters.** A specific road, river, or shipping lane that the world's economy depends on. Wars are fought over it. Heroes travel along it. Whoever closes it changes everything.
- **Show the financial elite.** Most fantasy power-brokers are crowns and swords. Add a Medici, a Fugger, a Rothschild equivalent. Bankers who can break kings.
- **Build the underclass economy.** The grey market, the black market, the patronage networks, the informal favors-and-debts that move a substantial share of any real economy.

## Common pitfalls

- **The medieval price-list.** D&D-style price lists for goods that don't reflect actual scarcity in the world being built. Question every default.
- **Economy as flavor only.** Mentioning markets and merchants without thinking through how anything actually flows.
- **Static economy.** Real economies have booms and busts, debasements, bubbles, crashes, gluts and shortages. Even a sketched economy should have *trends*.
- **Plot-convenient scarcity.** "It's a rare and precious metal" — but found at every plot-relevant moment. Pick a real scarcity story.
