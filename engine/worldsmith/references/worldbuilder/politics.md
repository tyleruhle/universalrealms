# Politics & Governance

Politics is how a society resolves the question *who decides?* Every system has answers — even anarchies and theocracies — and those answers shape almost everything else. Build the political system specifically, not as "a kingdom" or "a republic" with no further detail.

## The three core questions

For any polity, answer:

1. **Who's the ruler, formally?** (Monarch, council, assembly, priesthood, CEO, AI, no one?)
2. **Who's the ruler, actually?** (Often very different. The crown wears the king, sometimes.)
3. **What stops the ruler from doing whatever they want?** (Law, custom, military, money, faith, fear, foreign powers, the people, internal rivals?)

The gap between formal and actual power is where most political drama lives.

## Forms of government — and what they really are

Beyond textbook labels, what matters is *who has leverage*. A few useful archetypes:

- **Absolute monarchy** — one ruler nominally has all power. In practice always constrained by nobles, generals, priests, and the need to be obeyed.
- **Feudal hierarchy** — nested loyalties. The king commands dukes who command counts who command knights. The king's actual reach is limited by every link.
- **Constitutional monarchy** — ruler exists but power lies in a parliament or council.
- **Republic / oligarchy** — power formally distributed; in practice usually concentrated in a small elite (Roman senate, Venetian council, Renaissance Florence).
- **Direct democracy** — citizens decide directly. Almost always with a restricted definition of "citizen."
- **Theocracy** — religious authorities rule. Often layered over another structure (a king who's also high priest; a priesthood that controls a puppet king).
- **Tribal / clan** — extended kin networks; chiefs by acclaim, age, or inheritance; council of elders.
- **Stateless / acephalous** — no formal central authority. Order maintained by custom, mutual obligation, blood feud, market. Real societies have done this at scale.
- **Empire** — multiethnic polity, often with very different governance for the core vs. provinces.
- **Confederation** — autonomous polities cooperating loosely. Tensions over how much to centralize.

Don't just pick a label. Specify the texture: *who reports to whom, who appoints whom, who can remove whom, who taxes whom*.

## Sources of legitimacy

A government rules because most people accept that it should rule. The "because" varies:

- **Divine right** — the gods chose them (or this dynasty, or this office)
- **Bloodline** — inherited claim, sometimes traced to a founder
- **Conquest / right of arms** — we hold it because we took it
- **Tradition** — it's always been this way
- **Consent / election** — the people / the assembly chose
- **Competence** — we rule because we're competent (technocracy, examination systems)
- **Wealth** — we rule because we can afford to
- **Charisma** — a singular figure whose personal authority underwrites the office

Legitimacy can be layered. A Chinese emperor was simultaneously holder of the Mandate of Heaven (divine), of a dynastic claim (blood), of an examination-system bureaucracy (competence), and (when things were stable) of long tradition. When legitimacy frays, regimes fall.

## The administrative layer

Most fantasy politics jump straight from king to peasant and forget the entire administrative class — the people who actually make the state work. Build at least sketches of:

- **Tax collectors** — how is revenue raised? Land? Trade? Tribute? Head tax?
- **Judges / magistrates** — how are disputes resolved at the local level?
- **Constables / sheriffs** — who maintains order day-to-day?
- **Clerks / scribes** — who keeps the records that make all of this possible?
- **Officers / military** — career soldiers, levies, mercenaries, knights?
- **The professional class** — doctors, engineers, ritualists who serve the state

Bureaucracy isn't dry — it's where corruption, ambition, and reform actually happen. Some of history's most interesting political dramas are about a tax reform or a new examination system.

## Power dynamics — who actually decides what

For any decision the story cares about — a war, a marriage, a tax, an edict — work through:

- Who proposes it?
- Who must agree?
- Who can veto?
- Who can sabotage in implementation?
- Who can overturn it later?
- Whose informal consent matters even if not formally required?

This is often where a "simple decree from the king" turns out to involve negotiations with three dukes, the priesthood, the merchant houses funding the army, and the king's own mother.

## Succession

Succession crises are political dynamite. For any hereditary or office-based regime, decide:

- **What's the rule?** Primogeniture (eldest), ultimogeniture (youngest), agnatic (male-only), elective, selected by predecessor, by trial, by council vote?
- **What if the rule breaks down?** Disputed paternity, multiple legitimate heirs, an heir who's a child, an heir who's incompetent, an unpopular heir.
- **Who's positioned to exploit a crisis?** Pretenders, regents, neighboring powers, ambitious siblings.

A succession with a clean answer for every case is fragile worldbuilding. A succession with known weak points is *plot*.

For how real states answered these questions (tanistry, Ottoman fratricide and the *kafes*, the Venetian doge lottery, the Mandate of Heaven, clan mothers who could depose chiefs), see `references/worldbuilder/precedents.md` section 2. To watch a succession law play out over centuries, run it through saga.

## Law

Law in premodern settings is rarely a uniform code. Specify:

- **Sources of law:** royal decree, religious text, customary practice, court precedent?
- **Who's subject to which law?** Different rules often for clergy, nobles, foreigners, the unfree.
- **How are disputes settled?** Trial, arbitration, ordeal, duel, blood-payment, banishment?
- **What's the worst penalty?** Death (how administered?), maiming, slavery, exile, fines, shame?
- **What crimes does this law see that others wouldn't?** (Blasphemy? Adultery? Lese-majeste? Tax evasion?)
- **What crimes does this law *not* see?** (Violence within the family? Mistreatment of slaves? Crimes by nobles?)

Law reveals values. A law obsessed with sumptuary detail (who can wear what color) tells you about class anxiety. A law indifferent to murder among certain classes tells you who counts.

## International relations

If multiple polities exist, sketch the system:

- **Hierarchy:** is anyone hegemonic? Are there client states? Tributary relationships?
- **Diplomatic norms:** ambassadors? hostages? gift exchange? marriage alliances?
- **Casus belli:** what counts as a legitimate reason for war? Honor? Inheritance? Trade dispute? Religious offense?
- **Trade & treaties:** what's the framework — bilateral treaties, league memberships, religious umbrellas?
- **The barbarians:** every centralized polity defines an "other" outside the system. Who's that here, and is the characterization fair?

## Reform, revolt, revolution

Politics is dynamic. At any moment, there are forces pushing for change:

- **Reformers within the system** — improving without overthrowing
- **Reactionaries** — restoring the imagined better past
- **Revolutionaries** — overthrowing the system entirely
- **Separatists** — leaving the system for autonomy
- **Tax revolts / peasant uprisings** — usually about specific grievances, often co-optable
- **Court factions** — internal struggle that can become structural change

A polity with no internal pressure for change is unrealistic in the long run. Specify what's pushing on the current order even if no breakthrough is imminent.

## Common pitfalls

- **The king-and-peasant model.** Politics as a king deciding things and peasants experiencing the consequences. Build the middle.
- **Stable utopia or dystopia.** Both are unstable in reality. Even apparently stable orders are constantly being held in place by active work. Show the work.
- **Anachronistic ideology.** Modern political concepts (the nation-state, popular sovereignty, civil rights) projected onto premodern settings without grounding. If you use them, justify the divergence from historical premodernity.
- **No bureaucracy.** Empires don't run themselves. Show the clerks, the rolls, the seals, the system.
- **No mid-tier conflict.** Politics framed entirely as ruler vs. rebels, skipping the dukes, the cities, the guilds, the temples — the middle players who often decide everything.
- **Single-axis politics.** All conflicts are "for the throne" or "for independence." Real politics has many simultaneous axes — religious, economic, ethnic, generational, factional.

## Originality moves

- **Pick an unusual decision-making mechanism.** Selection by lottery (Athens). By divination. By auction. By trial-by-combat. By rotating chairmanship. By having to convince a sacred animal.
- **Decouple titles from power.** The king is ceremonial; real power is the Lord Treasurer. The Emperor is divine but cannot leave the temple compound. The "Council of Three" hasn't met in 80 years; everyone defers to the Permanent Secretariat.
- **Make legitimacy fragile in a specific way.** The current dynasty rules only because of a treaty that's almost expired. The Pope is uncontested because the only other claimant is mad — but is she?
- **Build the political calendar.** Each year, certain assemblies, oaths, taxes, rituals happen. The political world has a season; what's happening *this* season can shape any story.
- **Make the law specific and weird.** A law banning the wearing of red on the third day of the week. A law requiring magistrates to be bathed daily by their successors. Specificity makes law feel real.

## Output format

```
# Politics — [polity name]

## Form
Type, in textbook terms, with the asterisk explaining what's actually true.

## Legitimacy
Why people accept this rule (and where it's wearing thin).

## Structure
Ruler, council, ministers, bureaucracy, with appointment and removal rules.

## Real power
Who actually decides what — formal and informal.

## Succession
Rules, ambiguities, current line.

## Law
Sources, jurisdictions, key offenses and penalties.

## Administration
Tax, justice, military, records — who and how.

## International relations
Allies, enemies, tributaries, hostages.

## Pressures for change
Reform, revolt, court factions, foreign meddling.

## Open questions
```

For a world with multiple polities, write one of these per polity, and a top-level `politics.md` overview describing the system as a whole.
