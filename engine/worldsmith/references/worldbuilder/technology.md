# Technology

Technology is the set of tools, techniques, and infrastructure a civilization has available. It interacts with everything: war, economics, politics, daily life, magic. Many worlds get tech *level* approximately right and then ignore that tech *distribution*, *cost*, and *adjacent implications* are where the interesting work is.

## Tech level is too broad a category

The convenient labels — Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age, Medieval, Renaissance, Industrial, Information, Post-scarcity — are summaries, not specifications. Two worlds at "medieval" tech can be radically different.

Specify by *capability*, not era:

- Can they make steel? Cast iron? Just bronze? Just copper?
- Are there windmills? Watermills? Pumps?
- Is there mass-produced paper? Just parchment? Just oral tradition?
- Do they have printing? Movable type? Just woodblock?
- Have they navigation tools — compass, astrolabe, sextant, charts?
- Do they have firearms? At what stage — match? flint? cap? percussion? smokeless?

A world with steel, printing, but no firearms is different from one with firearms but no steel. Pick the actual checklist for your setting and stick to it.

## Tech is uneven

Real tech is distributed unequally across:

- **Region.** A trade-network capital has tools its hinterland villages don't.
- **Class.** A noble's house has artifacts the laborer's home doesn't, and vice versa (laborers often have superior craft tools).
- **Profession.** A surgeon has tools the farmer doesn't.
- **Period within a setting.** Some tech is rising (new), some falling (lost, forgotten).

"This world has clocks" is incomplete. Are clocks in every town square, or only in the imperial capital? Are mechanical clocks new and prestige, or old and ubiquitous? Are they declining because the makers are dying out?

## Infrastructure: the invisible technology

Most of what makes life modern isn't gadgets, it's infrastructure: roads, sanitation, water supply, postal systems, currency, weights and measures, law enforcement.

For any settled society, specify at sketch level:

- **Roads:** maintained or not? By whom? Tolled?
- **Water:** wells, aqueducts, springs? Are cities clean?
- **Waste:** where does it go? (Most premodern cities were appalling; some — Roman, Indus, Inca — were extraordinary.)
- **Information:** how do messages travel? Couriers, signal fires, posted notices, town criers, magical means?
- **Standards:** common weights/measures? Common time? Common currency or local?
- **Lighting:** torches, oil lamps, candles, gas, magical? Cities operate very differently after dark depending.

Infrastructure failure is plot fuel. A road washed out, a well poisoned, a courier killed — small infrastructural events have large consequences.

## Materials matter

Premodern history is often best understood as a materials history:

- **Wood:** the universal material; nearly everything begins as wood
- **Stone:** building, milling, weapons; varies enormously by what's local
- **Clay:** pottery, brick, tablets — cheap and abundant
- **Metals:** copper, tin (bronze), iron, steel — each step unlocks new capabilities
- **Fibers:** linen, wool, cotton, silk — clothing transforms society
- **Glass:** windows alone change architecture and life rhythm
- **Paper:** changes everything about knowledge and bureaucracy

For each major material, ask: where does it come from in this world, who controls it, who works it, and what does its availability or absence mean? A society with abundant cheap iron looks fundamentally different from one where iron is precious.

## Tech, war, and politics

New tech doesn't just give one side an advantage — it changes what war *is*.

- **Stirrup** → heavy cavalry → feudal aristocracy
- **Longbow / pike / firearm** → infantry defeats knights → centralized states with paid armies → end of feudalism
- **Cannon** → end of medieval walls → new defensive architecture (star forts) → siege warfare changes again
- **Railroad** → armies can be moved and supplied at unprecedented scale
- **Radio / telegraph** → command and control reach into the field

In any fantasy or sci-fi setting, ask: what's the dominant military technology, and what political structure does it support? When that tech is challenged, the politics will follow.

## Tech and magic

If both exist, decide their interaction. Possibilities:

- **Magic does what tech does, but better.** Magical lights replace lamps, magical communication replaces couriers. Tech is the poor person's magic. Implications: technology probably stagnates; magic users are an elite.
- **Magic does what tech can't.** Tech handles bulk; magic handles miracles. They coexist comfortably.
- **Magic is dangerous; tech is safe.** Tech advances rapidly because magic is risky.
- **Tech can replicate magic with effort.** Industrial magic — factories of spell-engines, alchemical assembly lines. Often interesting.
- **Magic and tech conflict.** Magic doesn't work near iron; tech disrupted in high-magic zones. Forces societies to choose, or specialize regionally.
- **Magic *is* the technology.** No mundane gunpowder; everything that looks like tech is magical. (Spelljammer, Eberron-like worlds.)

Whatever you pick, follow it through. A world where magic teleports goods cheaply will not have caravan trade. A world where magic heals reliably will not have apothecaries.

## The pace of change

Premodern tech changed slowly. A century could pass with little visible change in daily life. Modern (post-1750) tech accelerated dramatically. Future or alternate-history tech can do anything.

Set the pace in your world deliberately:

- **Static:** Tech has been the same for centuries. Why? (Sacred prohibition? Cyclical collapse? Inherent ceiling?)
- **Slowly improving:** Each generation slightly more capable than the last. Realistic baseline.
- **Punctuated:** Long stability, then a transformative breakthrough.
- **Rapid:** New things appearing within a single lifetime. Modern feel.
- **Lost.** Tech declining; the present has less than the past. Hugely evocative.
- **Catastrophic regression.** A specific event broke the old chain.

Each pace has different cultural implications. In a slow-changing world, master craftsmen carry centuries of accumulated wisdom. In a fast-changing world, expertise depreciates and there's a generation gap.

## Lost & forbidden tech

Lost or restricted tech is one of the highest-leverage worldbuilding moves:

- **Lost:** No one alive knows how to make it. (Greek fire, Roman concrete, certain dyes — all real examples.)
- **Suppressed:** Knowledge exists but is forbidden by religious, political, or magical authority.
- **Lost-to-them, kept-elsewhere:** One culture forgot what another preserved.
- **Forgotten-on-purpose:** A deliberate erasure that worked.
- **Locked behind initiation:** The knowledge exists but is held by a closed group.

Lost tech is plot gold: ruins to explore, fragments to translate, single surviving practitioners, factions racing to recover the secret.

## Common pitfalls

- **D&D static medieval.** Tech-frozen worlds where the same tech has worked for a thousand years for no reason. Why hasn't anything changed?
- **Tech as feature list.** "They have magic AND steam engines AND airships AND..." without thinking through interaction.
- **Single-culture tech.** All cultures in the world at the same tech level. Real world has had enormous tech disparities at any given moment.
- **The blank past.** A long history with no technological evolution mentioned. Even slowly-changing tech evolves; an empire that lasted 800 years should look different at year 800 than year 0.
- **Magitech inconsistency.** Magic that's "just like" technology except whenever it would be inconvenient for the plot.
- **Implausible tech-gaps within a society.** Spaceships but no plumbing. Castles with cell phones. These can work, but usually need explanation.

## Originality moves

- **Pick the limiting technology.** Most fantasy worlds gloss over agriculture. Lock down what fertilizer/irrigation/crop rotation is available, and population maxes follow.
- **Tech with cultural weight.** A craft so prestigious its masters wear specific dress and live in specific quarters. A technology only women practice, or only certain castes.
- **Make the breakthrough recent.** The printing press is 20 years old. Heavy cavalry charged its first battle within the protagonist's grandfather's lifetime. Tech that's new is conspicuous in a way ubiquitous tech isn't.
- **Combine periods deliberately.** A culture with bronze-age metalworking but Renaissance navigation and a printing press. Specify *why* — what was different about their history?
- **Forbidden technology with reasons.** Not "magic that's evil," but specific reasons: this technique once caused a catastrophe; the priesthood lost power when commoners learned this; the gods explicitly forbade this on a specific date.
- **Industrial fantasy.** Skip the medieval default. Build a world at 1750s tech with magic, or at 1920s, or at near-modern. Industrial revolutions with magical labor are nearly unexplored ground.
